Proposal for the CTO · Quality Engineering & MLOps

From AI-assisted to agentic quality engineering in 18 months

This plan moves DIRECTV's QA organisation from L1 to L4 across about 60 applications, and to a bounded L5 where the risk is low. At the same time it moves the testing work from IBM to DIRECTV's own team in Pune. We start with the five applications that matter most to revenue, prove the approach there in six months, and then bring the rest across in three waves. Each wave starts only when the one before it has hit its numbers.

How we get from 110 to 68

  • Agents write the first draft. Engineers review agent-written tests and triage instead of writing every test and reading every failure. That's what takes Tier 1 from about 2.4 people per app to 1.8.
  • Pool the low-risk apps. The 30 Tier 3 apps share one team of 8 instead of having owners spread thin.
  • Buy what's commodity. Self-healing, visual AI and a device cloud for streaming devices. The physical lab covers only receivers and Gemini.
  • Leaner shared teams. Each manager covers 12–15 people, and on-demand environments cut the manual environment work.

What 68 depends on

  • By the month 10 checkpoint, at least 60% of agent-drafted tests are accepted and triage is at least 85% accurate. If not, we hold at about 80 (US 9, India 71) until it is.
  • The tier mix is close to 10 / 20 / 30. Each extra Tier 1 app adds about 1.8 people.
  • Tool and device-cloud licences go up as headcount comes down. That cost is part of the month 1 business case.
  • Pune attrition of 12–15% a year is backfilled. That's an assumption about the Pune market.
  • The move to about 55 at L5 happens only when L5 has run for a full quarter without an escaped Sev-1.

8 · Levels

What each level means, and how we know an app has reached it

Automation percentage and AI maturity measure different things. With 90% automated regression at L1, the scripts exist, but people still decide what to test, write every test, read every failure and approve every release. Each level has an exit test that every app has to pass.

LevelWho does the workExit test for each app (all must pass)
L1 AI-assistedPeople, with an AI assistantAt least 80% of engineers use the approved AI assistant every week. There's a shared prompt library and a signed AI usage policy.
L2 AI-augmentedPeople, with tools that fix themselvesSelf-healing is on for all UI suites. Visual AI covers the top journeys. Flaky tests are under 3%. Synthetic test data is available on demand.
L3 AI-drivenAI decides what runs. People do the rest.AI test selection on every pull request catches at least 95% of what the full nightly run catches. A performance anomaly model gates builds. Every change gets a risk score.
L4 AgenticAgents write, run and triage. People approve.At least 50% of new tests are drafted by agents and accepted in review. At least 85% of failures are sorted correctly without a person. People approve only at set checkpoints.
L5 Autonomous (bounded)Rules and models release low-risk changesStable at L4 for 3+ months. In shadow mode, the agent's go/no-go matches the human decision at least 95% of the time. Automatic canary rollback is proven. Never used during a live-event change freeze.

10 · The shared platform

The quality intelligence platform

We build one platform in months 1–6 and reuse it for every later wave. That's what makes onboarding 15 apps in 4 months realistic. Use the buttons to zoom or go full screen.

Guardrails

AI models run inside DIRECTV's own cloud account. No customer personal data goes into prompts, and test data is synthetic. Every agent action is logged, and agents get only the access they need.

Build or buy

Buy the commodity pieces: self-healing, visual AI, device cloud. Build the models that learn from DIRECTV's own defects, incidents and telemetry, because nobody can sell us those.

MLOps standard

Every model has a versioned dataset, an evaluation gate, a drift monitor, a retraining trigger and a named owner. QE's own models follow the same rules as the product's.

11 · The AI in this plan

What we build, what we use, and where people stay in charge

The plan uses three kinds of AI. Mixing them up leads to the wrong cost and risk conversation, so here they are separately.

1. Models we train (M1–M10)

Small, focused models trained on DIRECTV's own test, defect and telemetry data, using open-source libraries such as LightGBM and XGBoost. They're cheap to run on ordinary servers, easy to explain, and nobody else has them.

2. Pre-trained open models we host

Large language models, embedding models and time-series models that we use as they are, without training them. We host them in DIRECTV's cloud and give them DIRECTV context through search over our own documents (RAG).

3. Agents (AG1–AG10)

An LLM connected to tools such as Jira, Git and the test runners, with tightly limited permissions. Each agent does one job and stops at a human checkpoint. Agents use the models from 1 and 2.

Pre-trained models the agents run on

Shortlist for the month 1 bake-off

  • Writing and fixing test code: Qwen3-Coder (Apache 2.0)
  • Reasoning (triage, test plans, release evidence): gpt-oss-120b (Apache 2.0)
  • High-volume, simple classification: gpt-oss-20b (Apache 2.0)
  • Search over test assets and RCAs: BGE-M3 embeddings (MIT)
  • Agent workflow and tooling: LangGraph (MIT), MLflow (Apache 2.0), Evidently for drift (Apache 2.0)

How we choose

  • All are open-weight and can run inside DIRECTV's cloud, so code and test data never leave it.
  • We test them on about 200 real DIRECTV tasks in month 1 and choose on measured results. Newer versions replace them as they come out.
  • If the CISO approves, a hosted frontier model from DIRECTV's existing cloud provider can handle the hardest tasks. Which providers DIRECTV already allows is unknown to me.

Option A · Self-hosted open-weight (where I'd start)

  • Code and test data never leave DIRECTV's account.
  • Predictable, fixed cost.
  • The hardest tasks may be slightly weaker than on the best hosted models.
  • Needs GPU servers and someone to run them. Roughly 4–8 data-centre GPUs including redundancy (my estimate, sized properly in the bake-off).

Option B · Hybrid

  • Self-hosted by default, plus a hosted frontier model from DIRECTV's existing cloud provider for the hardest 10–20% of tasks.
  • Best quality where it counts.
  • Pay-per-use cost, a second set of data rules, and CISO approval needed.

Option C · Hosted only

  • Fastest start, with no GPUs to run.
  • Data goes to a provider under contract.
  • Cost grows with usage, and it's harder to switch later.

Recommendation: start with option A and keep option B ready in case the bake-off shows a quality gap on the hardest tasks.

16 · Near-zero production issues and a loopback for every failover

Defining near-zero so we can measure it

Zero defects isn't a promise anyone can keep. Some outages can't be caught by testing at all, such as a third-party feed, a CDN, or rain fade on satellite. So near-zero is a set of numbers that QE, SRE and engineering own together.

MeasureProposed target (set after the baseline)
Sev-1 incidents caused by a defect that testing should have caught0 per quarter
Sev-2 escaped defects across the portfolioNo more than 1 a month
Change failure rate (DORA)Under 5%
Customer-facing incidents during the biggest live events0
Playback and entitlement availability99.95%
Incidents and failovers turned into an automated test within 5 working days100%

Prevent and detect

Risk-based test selection, performance gates, contract tests and 72-hour soak tests stop issues before release.

Contain and survive

Canary releases at 1% → 5% → 25% → 100%, rolled back automatically if the error budget starts burning. Feature flags. A tested failover path for every critical dependency.

Learn (the loopback)

Every Sev-1 or Sev-2 and every production failover produces a test that reproduces it, a chaos experiment, and a new training label for the risk model.

20 · Questions you may have

Likely questions, with straight answers

Can the most critical apps really reach L4 in six months?

The six-month claim covers five apps, not the whole portfolio. Months 3 and 4 run in shadow, so no existing gate is removed until the model has beaten it. L4 has a measurable exit test, and if one app misses, only that app gets more time.

Is the 90% automation figure real?

We'll know in month 1. Counting tests can hide gaps in what's actually covered, so we re-measure by journey and risk. If the real figure is lower, month 2 grows and we'll say so at the month 1 gate.

Why not move everything to Pune in one go?

IBM's people hold the knowledge of 60 applications. A single cutover during sports season is the most likely way to cause the outages we're trying to prevent. Each wave moves through its own shadow and reverse shadow, and IBM leaves a wave only after Pune passes the gate.

Will AI release software on its own during a live game?

No. L5 covers only defined low-risk change types, starts only after 95% agreement in shadow, needs automatic canary rollback, and is switched off during event freezes. People own every high-risk release.

Satellite is shrinking. Why spend on receiver testing?

It still serves a large share of customers, though the exact split isn't public, and one bad firmware update could reach a big part of that base at once. Most of the investment goes to Gemini, which serves both satellite and internet customers.

What will it cost?

A credible number needs IBM's rates and the tool licence costs, which I don't have. The month 1 deliverable is a costed business case: Pune hires, AI model hosting, device cloud and tools, set against the IBM spend that goes away. On headcount alone, the team falls from about 110 to 68 by month 18.

Decisions for the first 30 days

These are the decisions that let month 1 start on time.

  • Confirm the top 5 applications and the start date.
  • Agree, with the CISO, which AI models can be used and where data may go.
  • Approve the target team of 68 (8 in the US, 60 in Pune), with about 80 as the safety net if the month 10 checkpoint isn't met. Release the first 26 Pune requisitions, plus interim specialists for months 1–3.
  • Fund the transition overlap (a peak of about 136 people around month 6) and the US device and receiver lab.
  • Give procurement a mandate to agree IBM's exit schedule and knowledge-transfer duties.
  • Agree the shared near-zero targets with SRE and engineering leadership.
  • Grant read access to CI/CD, Jira, Performance Center, APM and streaming telemetry for the baseline.
  • Agree the gate model: no wave starts until the one before it passes its exit test.
